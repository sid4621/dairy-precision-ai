AI-Based Predictive Modelling of Milk Production and Health Parameters in Dairy Cattle
14 April 2026
AI-based predictive modelling in the dairy industry focuses on improving operational efficiency by accurately forecasting milk yields and detecting health issues like mastitis or lameness up to a week before clinical symptoms appear. By integrating diverse data streams from wearable sensors, milking robots, and thermal cameras, these models enable a shift from reactive treatments to proactive health management.

Key Predictive Models
Milk Production Prediction:
Random Forest (RF): Widely used for its ability to handle complex, non-linear relationships and missing data. One study achieved an

Neural Networks (MLP & LSTM): These models often show optimal stability and accuracy. Research indicates that combining Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLP) and Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks can reach an

nextMILK Model: A specialized RF model that uses previous lactation data to predict "healthy" yield residuals (MRT) in current lactations, helping identify metabolic success during the critical transition period.
Health & Disease Detection:
Mastitis Prediction: AI models analyze daily production indicators (like milk flow rate and conductivity) and thermal images to detect infections. Multi-modal frameworks have achieved up to 91.6% accuracy for early-stage disease detection.
Metritis & Lameness: Neural networks and Support Vector Machines (SVM) are preferred for detecting these conditions. For instance, NN models have shown 96.1% accuracy in early metritis detection by monitoring rumination time and body weight.

To develop a high-performing milk yield prediction model, you do not have to limit yourself to a single dataset. In fact, combining data from multiple sources (known as data fusion) is the modern standard for achieving high accuracy and generalizability.

While you can start with one dataset for a proof-of-concept, here is how you should decide between using one or multiple datasets:
1. When to use a single dataset
Using one particular dataset (e.g., from a single farm or a specific study) is best for niche optimization: 
Specific Farm Management: If your goal is to build a tool for a specific farm, the model should prioritize that farm’s unique data (breed, feed types, and climate) to ensure local accuracy.
Initial Prototyping: It is easier to clean and preprocess data from a single source where the sensor formats and logging intervals are consistent.
Breed-Specific Models: Some research focuses on a single breed (like Holstein or Sahiwal) to capture precise biological traits without the "noise" of multi-breed variations.

2. When to use multiple datasets
Using multiple datasets is the superior approach if you want to develop a robust, commercial-grade tool: 
Improved Accuracy: Combining sensor data (wearables) with environmental data (weather stations) and historical logs has been shown to increase prediction accuracy by up to 20%.
Generalizability: Models trained on single-farm data often fail when moved to a different geographic region or a different production system (e.g., pasture-based vs. indoor). Using multiple datasets helps the AI "learn" beyond the limitations of one environment.
Filling Data Gaps: If one dataset lacks weather information and another lacks health records, merging them allows the model to learn complex relationships, such as how heat stress (
) impacts yield during specific disease events like mastitis.

Yes, several high-quality datasets specifically document how milk yield is impacted when dairy cattle are unhealthy. These datasets are often used to build "resilience" indicators or early-warning systems for diseases like mastitis, lameness, and ketosis. 
Top Datasets for Health-Impact Modeling
Exploring Milk Loss and Variability (Frontiers in Genetics): This comprehensive dataset covers 8,935 lactations from 6,816 Chinese Holstein cows. It defines "milk loss traits" by measuring negative deviations from an expected lactation curve. It found that cows experience an average of 3.73 perturbations per lactation, with an average milk loss of 267 kg per cow.
Source: Available via the study Exploring milk loss and variability during environmental perturbations.
MmCows (Multimodal Dairy Cattle Dataset): A highly detailed dataset featuring multiple sensing modalities (UWB location, core body temperature, activity) alongside daily milk yield. It is used to show how behavior changes—like increased standing during heat stress or abnormal gait in lameness—directly correlate with decreased production.
Source: MmCows Dataset on Kaggle.
Decoding Onset and Recovery (Mastitis Impact): This retrospective study utilized data from 11 dairy farms (Spain, UK, Brazil) with 885,759 individual milk records. It specifically assesses milk yield depression in 3,473 cases of mild and severe mastitis, showing average production drops from ~35 kg/d to as low as 29.4 kg/d for severe cases.
Source: ScienceDirect: Decoding onset and recovery patterns.
Cow Mastitis (From milk) - Kaggle: A dedicated dataset for detecting mastitis using udder sensor measurements and body temperature. It provides a direct binary target (healthy vs. affected) to train predictive models on how these health indicators change milk quality and quantity.
Source: Kaggle: Cow Mastitis Detection.
